Insurance and Banking Law

2019 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Bir Singh v Mukesh Kumar

A voluntarily signed blank cheque may be completed by the holder without becoming invalid merely for that reason; once foundational execution is shown, statutory presumptions arise, but the accused may rebut them on the applicable standard.

It connects negotiability, presumptions and evidential rebuttal.

2014 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Export Credit Guarantee Corporation of India Ltd v Garg Sons International

Courts interpret the contract but do not make a new policy for the parties; clear limitations and conditions must be applied, subject to statute and recognised interpretive principles.

It is useful for the boundary between interpretation and rewriting an insurance bargain.

2012 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Aneeta Hada v Godfather Travels and Tours Pvt Ltd

Where liability is founded on the statutory vicarious-liability provision, arraigning and establishing the offence of the company is ordinarily a necessary condition before responsible officers can be held liable, subject to legally recognised exceptions such as impossibility.

It is the anchor for entity-first analysis in cheque-dishonour company cases.

Rangappa v Sri Mohan

The presumption includes the existence of a legally enforceable debt or liability, but the accused may rebut it on the civil standard through the record and circumstances.

It is a principal authority on cheque-dishonour presumptions and burden shifting.

2004 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

United India Insurance Co Ltd v Harchand Rai Chandan Lal

The court must give effect to the policy as written, reading coverage and exclusions together; sympathy cannot create a risk the parties did not insure, although ambiguity and consumer-law principles may affect other cases.

It is a central reminder that insurance liability begins with the precise insured peril.

2002 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Central Bank of India v Ravindra

Interest may be capitalised only according to contract, banking practice and law; penal interest cannot ordinarily be capitalised in the same way, and courts must distinguish pre-suit, pendente lite and post-decree interest.

It is the leading conceptual map for interest calculations in banking litigation.

Life Insurance Corporation of India v Consumer Education and Research Centre

The Court treated life insurance as connected with social security and held that a public insurer's terms must satisfy fairness and non-arbitrariness.

Canara Bank v Canara Sales Corporation

A forged cheque carries no customer mandate, so the bank generally cannot debit the account unless the customer's conduct creates a legally recognised defence.

It is the anchor for bank mandate, forgery and duty of care.

General Assurance Society Ltd v Chandmull Jain

The policy must be read as a whole according to its words; courts cannot add terms, though ambiguity may be construed against the drafter in an appropriate case.

It is a classic Indian authority on policy construction.
