2026 · Supreme Court of India · 2026 INSC 668

Pooja Ramesh Singh v Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd

The Court set aside the tainted decisions, held that a decision infected by fabricated precedent cannot stand, adopted zero tolerance for unverified hallucinated authority and directed the Bar Council of India to consider professional standards.

It is a crucial current case for legal research, citation verification, judicial reasoning and professional responsibility.

2017 · Supreme Court of India · [2017] 10 SCR 569

Justice K S Puttaswamy (Retd) v Union of India

The Court unanimously recognised privacy as an intrinsic part of life, liberty and the freedoms in Part III, while explaining that restrictions require legality, legitimate aim and proportional safeguards.

It supplies the constitutional foundation for bodily autonomy, informational privacy, dignity, sexuality and data governance.

1978 · Supreme Court of India · [1978] 2 SCR 621

Maneka Gandhi v Union of India

The Court held that procedure under Article 21 cannot be arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; it must satisfy fairness and be tested with Articles 14 and 19 where engaged. Natural-justice requirements may be implied even when a statute is silent.

It transformed Indian rights review, administrative fairness and the constitutional meaning of personal liberty.

1976 · Supreme Court of India · (1976) 2 SCC 521

Additional District Magistrate Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla

The majority denied the remedy; Justice H R Khanna dissented. The majority position was later expressly disapproved and treated as overruled in the privacy judgment.

It is a leading negative precedent on emergency power, judicial role and the rule of law.

1973 · Supreme Court of India · [1973] Supp SCR 1

His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v State of Kerala

Parliament's amending power is wide but cannot damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure. The opinions differed on detail, but the basic-structure limitation became the controlling proposition.

It is the central authority for limited constitutional amendment, constitutional identity and the difference between constituent power and ordinary legislative power.

1970 · Supreme Court of India · (1970) 1 SCC 248

R C Cooper v Union of India

The Court focused on the direct effect of the measure and rejected rigid compartmentalisation of fundamental rights.

It forms the bridge from A K Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi and is central to effects-based constitutional analysis.

A K Kraipak v Union of India

The Court rejected a rigid administrative/quasi-judicial divide and applied fairness and bias principles to administrative decision-making.

1967 · Supreme Court of India · AIR 1967 SC 1643

I C Golak Nath v State of Punjab

The majority held that Parliament could not abridge fundamental rights and used prospective overruling. The amendment-power proposition was later displaced by Kesavananda Bharati, though the case remains important for prospective overruling.

It is a crucial doctrinal step between Shankari Prasad and Kesavananda.

1950 · Supreme Court of India · [1950] SCR 88

A K Gopalan v State of Madras

The majority treated the rights as largely separate and read 'procedure established by law' narrowly. The compartmental approach was displaced by later decisions, especially R C Cooper and Maneka Gandhi.

It is indispensable as a historical starting point for the evolution of Article 21 and for understanding how precedent can be narrowed or overruled.

1927 · Permanent Court of International Justice · PCIJ Series A No 10

S S Lotus (France v Turkey)

The Court adopted a permissive approach: restrictions on state jurisdiction cannot simply be presumed, though later law and treaties qualify the decision's broad language.

It is a classic starting point for jurisdiction, sovereignty and the evolution of international law.
