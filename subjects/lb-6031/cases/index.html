Interpretation of Statutes

2018 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Commissioner of Customs (Import), Mumbai v Dilip Kumar and Company

The claimant must establish that the exemption applies; unresolved ambiguity in an exemption notification is ordinarily construed in favour of the revenue, while ambiguity in the charging provision follows different principles.

It is the principal modern authority distinguishing charging provisions from exemption claims.

2013 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Rohitash Kumar v Om Prakash Sharma

Courts cannot ordinarily supply an omission or recast legislation merely because the result seems inconvenient; correction is exceptional and must be compelled by the statute read as a whole.

It is an accessible modern statement of the limits of judicial supplementation.

2005 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Nathi Devi v Radha Devi Gupta

Where language is clear and produces a workable result, the court applies it; purpose and context assist interpretation but do not authorise addition or subtraction of words.

It is useful for literal meaning, whole-statute reading and the boundary of judicial construction.

2005 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

State of Jharkhand v Govind Singh

Judicial interpretation cannot add words or create an offence that the legislature did not enact; an apparent gap must ordinarily be left for legislative correction.

It demonstrates the separation-of-powers reason behind the casus-omissus rule.

1987 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Reserve Bank of India v Peerless General Finance and Investment Co Ltd

Interpretation depends on the text and context together; a provision should be read as part of the entire statute, its scheme and purpose rather than as an isolated sentence.

It is a concise anchor for whole-statute and contextual interpretation.

K P Varghese v Income Tax Officer

The Court used text, statutory purpose and legislative history to require proof of understatement rather than taxing a fictional receipt.

B Shah v Presiding Officer, Labour Court, Coimbatore

Bengal Immunity Co Ltd v State of Bihar

1954 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Tolaram Relumal v State of Bombay

A penal provision should not be extended by implication; where two reasonable constructions remain after ordinary interpretation, the one avoiding penal liability is preferred.

It is a classic anchor for strict construction of penal statutes.
