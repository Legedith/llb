2026 · Supreme Court of India · 2026 INSC 668

Pooja Ramesh Singh v Jammu and Kashmir Bank Ltd

The Court set aside the tainted decisions, held that a decision infected by fabricated precedent cannot stand, adopted zero tolerance for unverified hallucinated authority and directed the Bar Council of India to consider professional standards.

It is a crucial current case for legal research, citation verification, judicial reasoning and professional responsibility.

2019 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

M Siddiq (D) through LRs v Mahant Suresh Das

The Court treated the deity as a juristic person, assessed documentary and archaeological evidence, distinguished faith from proof of title, and fashioned relief through the law of property and constitutional authority.

It is a rich case study in legal personality, evidence, possession and remedial design.

2017 · Uttarakhand High Court · Citation to verify

Mohd Salim v State of Uttarakhand

The High Court used juristic personality as a protective device, but the order's operation and practical implications were questioned and stayed in later Supreme Court proceedings. It should be studied as an ambitious but unsettled personhood experiment.

It is valuable precisely because it shows both the attraction and institutional limits of nature personhood.

2014 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Animal Welfare Board of India v A Nagaraja

Animal-welfare statutes must be applied to prevent unnecessary pain and protect animal well-being; constitutional duties and an ecocentric approach may inform interpretation, although the precise constitutional status of animal rights remains doctrinally contested and later legislation must be checked.

It is a major bridge between legal personhood debates, statutory welfare and constitutional duties.

2011 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

K T Plantation Pvt Ltd v State of Karnataka

Deprivation of property requires authority of valid law; public purpose, non-arbitrariness and the character of compensation may be relevant to constitutional review, although Article 300A is not identical to the former fundamental right.

It connects ownership theory to the modern constitutional status of property.

Ram Jankijee Deities v State of Bihar

The juristic deity acts through a human representative, and courts distinguish the legal person, the physical idol and the manager who protects the endowed property.

It sharpens the institutional mechanics of non-natural legal personality.

1986 · Supreme Court of India · (1986) 1 SCC 264

Life Insurance Corporation of India v Escorts Ltd

The Court recognised that veil lifting is exceptional and depends on statute, fraud, evasion, agency or the demands of the particular legal rule; it is not a general licence to ignore incorporation.

It is a leading Indian statement of veil-piercing limits.

1973 · Supreme Court of India · [1973] Supp SCR 1

His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v State of Kerala

Parliament's amending power is wide but cannot damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure. The opinions differed on detail, but the basic-structure limitation became the controlling proposition.

It is the central authority for limited constitutional amendment, constitutional identity and the difference between constituent power and ordinary legislative power.

1969 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Yogendra Nath Naskar v Commissioner of Income Tax

A consecrated deity may be recognised as a juristic person capable of holding property and being represented through a human manager, with legal personality serving the purposes of the endowment.

It is a foundational Indian example of functional juristic personality.
