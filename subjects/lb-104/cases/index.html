The Court invalidated section 377 to the extent it criminalised consensual adult intimacy, while leaving non-consensual acts and acts involving minors outside that protection.

The Court read down the exception so that intercourse with a wife below eighteen falls within the rape offence, harmonising criminal, child-protection and marriage laws.

Darshan Singh v State of Punjab

The Court summarised that private defence is preventive, assessed realistically rather than with golden scales, but force must remain connected to a reasonable apprehension and statutory limits.

Criminal negligence requires a very high degree of grossness; professional conduct is assessed against an ordinarily competent practitioner, and prosecution should not be launched casually.

Conversion does not automatically dissolve the subsisting marriage; a second marriage during its continuance can attract bigamy consequences.

Tukaram v State of Maharashtra

The majority upheld acquittal on the then record and legal framework. The decision became a major negative precedent that triggered statutory reforms on consent, custody and evidential burdens.

It is historically essential for understanding rape-law reform and the dangers of equating submission with consent.

State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George

The Court upheld liability in the statutory setting, treating the offence as one where the legislative scheme displaced the ordinary mens rea presumption.

The Court held that the sequence and interval showed deliberation rather than a legally sufficient continuing provocation, and restored the murder conviction.

1958 · Supreme Court of India · AIR 1958 SC 465

Virsa Singh v State of Punjab

The prosecution must prove the bodily injury, that it was intentionally inflicted, and that the injury objectively was sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; intent to cause death is not separately required under that clause.

It is the classic element-by-element explanation of murder based on intended injury.

Basdev v State of Pepsu

Voluntary intoxication generally does not excuse; knowledge may be imputed as if sober, while the prosecution must still prove any specific intention required by the offence.

It is the standard Indian authority on voluntary intoxication.
