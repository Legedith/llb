2020 · Supreme Court of India · (2020) 2 SCC 617

Keisham Meghachandra Singh v Hon'ble Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly

The Court directed time-bound adjudication and suggested reconsideration of the Speaker's institutional role, reflecting concern about strategic delay.

It is a major current application of Kihoto Hollohan.

2017 · Supreme Court of India · (2017) 3 SCC 1

Krishna Kumar Singh v State of Bihar

The Court condemned routine re-promulgation as a fraud on the Constitution, required legislative accountability and rejected any automatic theory of enduring rights after lapse.

It is the modern anchor on ordinance power, legislative control and constitutional accountability.

2002 · Supreme Court of India · Citation to verify

Rupa Ashok Hurra v Ashok Hurra

To prevent a gross miscarriage of justice in exceptional circumstances, the Court recognised a tightly confined curative petition following review, subject to certification, circulation and demanding threshold conditions.

It is the foundation of curative jurisdiction and finality safeguards.

1994 · Supreme Court of India · [1994] 2 SCR 644

S R Bommai v Union of India

The Court held that Article 356 proclamations are reviewable, majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House, and secularism is a basic constitutional feature.

It is the principal authority on President's Rule, federalism, floor tests and secular constitutional government.

1992 · Supreme Court of India · [1992] 1 SCR 686

Kihoto Hollohan v Zachillhu

The Court largely upheld the Tenth Schedule while holding that final decisions of the Speaker are subject to judicial review on recognised grounds.

It is the starting point for anti-defection adjudication and the constitutional status of the Speaker.

1987 · Supreme Court of India · (1987) 1 SCC 378

D C Wadhwa v State of Bihar

The Court held that re-promulgation subverts the constitutional scheme and cannot become a substitute for legislative enactment.

It is the foundational ordinance-abuse case later developed by Krishna Kumar Singh.

1980 · Supreme Court of India · [1981] 1 SCR 206

Minerva Mills Ltd v Union of India

The Court held that limited amending power and the harmony between fundamental rights and Directive Principles are features of the basic structure.

It explains why neither rights nor social-policy directives can be treated as constitutionally absolute.

1978 · Supreme Court of India · [1978] 2 SCR 621

Maneka Gandhi v Union of India

The Court held that procedure under Article 21 cannot be arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; it must satisfy fairness and be tested with Articles 14 and 19 where engaged. Natural-justice requirements may be implied even when a statute is silent.

It transformed Indian rights review, administrative fairness and the constitutional meaning of personal liberty.

Additional District Magistrate Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla

The majority denied the remedy; Justice H R Khanna dissented. The majority position was later expressly disapproved and treated as overruled in the privacy judgment.

Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain

The Court invalidated the immunity provision through basic-structure reasoning, emphasising free and fair elections, equality and judicial review.

1973 · Supreme Court of India · [1973] Supp SCR 1

His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v State of Kerala

Parliament's amending power is wide but cannot damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure. The opinions differed on detail, but the basic-structure limitation became the controlling proposition.

It is the central authority for limited constitutional amendment, constitutional identity and the difference between constituent power and ordinary legislative power.

1970 · Supreme Court of India · (1970) 1 SCC 248

R C Cooper v Union of India

The Court focused on the direct effect of the measure and rejected rigid compartmentalisation of fundamental rights.

It forms the bridge from A K Gopalan to Maneka Gandhi and is central to effects-based constitutional analysis.
